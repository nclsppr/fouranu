## Choose it for the fire, not the specification sheet

Choose the Karu 12 if you genuinely want to learn how to feed a fire. That is
what makes it interesting. It takes wood and charcoal, while a separately sold
burner lets you switch to propane. This fallback is practical, but it does not
turn the oven into an effortless appliance.

If you mainly want to ignite the oven, turn a dial and bake, a model designed
for gas will be more coherent. Our guide to
[gas, electric or multi-fuel ovens](/en/ooni/gas-electric-or-multi-fuel/) will
help you settle that point before comparing individual models.

## Compact on paper, less so once installed

The Karu 12 is designed for 30 cm pizzas. It weighs 12 kg, has a 15 mm stone
and measures 80 × 40 × 77 cm according to the published specifications. That
format remains reasonable on a terrace.

You still need to account for the flue, the fuel and the room required to work
around the oven. One line of dimensions does not show all that working space.

Ooni states a maximum temperature of 500°C, 450°C in 15 minutes and a pizza in
60 seconds. These are manufacturer reference points, not a guaranteed result at
home. They specify neither the amount of wood, the weather, the measurement
point on the stone nor the dough used. An
[infrared thermometer](/en/pizza-accessories/infrared-thermometer-guide/) at
least lets you return to the same point at home before every launch.

| Useful figure | Published value | What it does not tell you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pizza | 30 cm | The room available to turn the pizza |
| Weight | 12 kg | The weight of the burner, fuel and tools |
| Stone | 15 mm | Recovery between several pizzas |
| Dimensions | 80 × 40 × 77 cm | Safety clearances and the working area |

## Three fuels, but one main use

Wood and charcoal require preparing the fire, monitoring its strength and
managing the residue. Propane removes part of that ritual but adds a burner and
its connection. You will probably use the same fuel most of the time even if
the oven accepts several.

Our advice is clear. Do not buy the Karu 12 simply for the satisfaction of
ticking three fuels. Buy it if cooking over a fire genuinely appeals to you.
Versatility then becomes a useful safety net. Otherwise, it risks remaining a
feature you paid for and rarely use.

## Who it suits

The Karu 12 makes sense for someone looking for a compact format, with suitable
outdoor space and a willingness to experiment while learning to manage the
fire. It can also suit you if you are still undecided about fuel and keep the
gas burner as an option for later.

Rule it out for an indoor kitchen, a very tight terrace or service where pizzas
need to follow one another without watching the flame. Also look at the
[Karu 2](/en/ooni/karu-2/) before deciding.

## Five checks before paying

- Where will the wood or charcoal be stored, dry and away from heat?
- Does the table leave enough room for the flue and your serving movements?
- Are the propane burner, regulator and cover included in your budget?
- How many pizzas do you want to bake in succession?
- Are you prepared to clean out the ash after the evening?

## What is still missing

No usable third-party measurement lets us establish a heating time,
consumption figure or the speed at which the stone regains heat between two
pizzas. We therefore cannot present the Karu 12 as the champion of wood-fired
cooking or as a particularly fast oven.

The useful conclusion fits in one sentence. Its format and constraints are
known; its performance remains to be documented. First decide whether you
genuinely want to cook over a fire.
